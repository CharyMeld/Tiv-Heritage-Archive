## muhammadu-attahiru-i (+201 words)

**career:**

> Attahiru came to the throne in October 1902, on the death of Sultan Abderrahman dan Abi Bakar, when British forces had already taken parts of the caliphate; Frederick Lugard had used rivalries between the southern emirates and Sokoto to prevent a united defence (Wikipedia). As a British-led force approached Sokoto, Attahiru organised a hasty defence and fought outside the city, but British firepower won quickly and caused heavy losses among his men.
>
> After leaving Sokoto he travelled through the countryside gathering supporters, pursued by the British. The British and the emirs who worked with them were alarmed by the numbers who joined him; his following grew to thousands as it moved through Zamfara and Kano, and Wikipedia's article on the Sokoto Caliphate records Muslim supporters coming from Hausaland, Segu, Massina and Adamawa towards Burmi, on the far eastern border of the caliphate.

**historical_significance:**

> After his death the British displayed photographs of his severed head across Northern Nigeria to "convince the diehards of the futility of fighting" (Wikipedia). Lugard kept the title of sultan as a symbolic position in the new protectorate. Attahiru's son, Muhammad Bello bin Attahiru (Mai Wurno), led the remaining followers to Sudan, where many of their descendants still live.

## gambo-sawaba (+265 words)

**early_life:**

> Her father, a graduate of the Ghana School of Survey, came to Nigeria in 1910 to seek work with the railway; her mother, Fatima, was a widow from Lavun in Niger State. Sawaba, named Hajaratu, was the fifth of their six children. She attended the Native Authority primary school in Tudun Wada, but had to leave school after her father died in 1943; her mother died three years later. At thirteen she was married to a Second World War veteran who left after her first pregnancy, and her later marriages did not last. As a child she was known for defending weaker friends in street fights, saying, "OK, I have bought the fight from you" (Wikipedia).

**career:**

> She was involved in politics from the age of seventeen, joining the opposition Northern Elements Progressive Union at a time when the Northern People's Congress, backed by the emirs and the colonial authorities, dominated the north. She campaigned against under-age marriage and forced labour and for Western education in the north, and made her name by standing up to speak at a political meeting full of men. Her mentor, Aminu Kano, gave her the name Sawaba, meaning freedom or redemption, after she was elected head of NEPU's women's wing; she was also mentored by Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti (Wikipedia).

**legacy:**

> She is widely regarded as a pioneer of the struggle for the liberation of northern women, and was described as "the most jailed Nigerian female"; she kept a blanket marked "Prison Yard" for her arrests. A general hospital in Kaduna and a hostel at Bayero University, Kano, are named after her (Wikipedia).

## umaru-musa-yaradua (+190 words)

**education:**

> Yar'Adua began school at Rafukka Primary School in 1958, moved to Dutsinma Boarding Primary School in 1962, attended Government College, Keffi, from 1965 to 1969, and gained a Higher School Certificate at Barewa College in 1971. He studied education and chemistry at Ahmadu Bello University, Zaria, from 1972 to 1975, and later returned for a master's degree in analytical chemistry (Wikipedia).

**career:**

> After national service as a teacher in Lagos he lectured in Katsina from 1976 to 1983. At university he was active in radical circles around the Marxist historian Yusufu Bala Usman, and in the Second Republic joined the People's Redemption Party. He was later general manager of Sambo Farms in Funtua and a director of several companies. During Babangida's transition he was a founding member of the Peoples Front of Nigeria, led by his elder brother, Major General Shehu Musa Yar'Adua, which became part of the Social Democratic Party; he lost the 1991 Katsina governorship election. His brother died in prison under Abacha. In 1998 he formed the K34 association, which joined others to form the People's Democratic Party, and he was elected governor of Katsina in 1999 (Wikipedia).

## dora-akunyili (+180 words)

**education:**

> She went to St Patrick's Primary School, Isuofia, and Queen of the Rosary Secondary School, Nsukka, where she passed with distinction in 1973 and won scholarships from the Eastern Nigerian government and the federal government. She studied pharmacy at the University of Nigeria, graduating in 1978, and earned a PhD in ethnopharmacology in 1985 (Wikipedia). She worked as a hospital pharmacist at the University of Nigeria Teaching Hospital in Enugu, lectured at the university, becoming a senior lecturer in 1990, and from 1996 was zonal secretary of the Petroleum Special Trust Fund for the south-eastern states. President Olusegun Obasanjo appointed her to head NAFDAC in 2001.

**achievements:**

> In July 2003, after four children died during heart operations in Enugu, she confiscated the supplies used and found fake adrenaline, fake muscle relaxant and infected drips. Her work raised public awareness of counterfeit medicines and tightened surveillance at Nigerian customs. On 26 December 2003 gunmen fired on her convoy in Anambra State; a bullet passed through her headscarf. She received several international awards for her work in public health and human rights (Wikipedia).

## abubakar-tafawa-balewa (+158 words)

**career:**

> Balewa entered politics in the late 1940s as Bauchi's representative in the Northern House of Assembly, and in 1949 became an education officer. When the Northern People's Congress was formed in 1951 he joined it; in 1952 he moved to Lagos as a member of the central House of Representatives and became minister of works, and under the 1954 constitution he was one of three northern members of the Council of Ministers and minister of transport. In 1954 he lost the party leadership election to Ahmadu Bello and became vice-president of the NPC. In 1957 he became chief minister and prime minister-designate, and formed an all-party government that included the NCNC and the Action Group (Wikipedia).

**historical_significance:**

> On 18 January 1959 he moved the motion in Parliament asking Britain to establish the Federation of Nigeria as an independent sovereign state on 1 October 1960. Wikipedia describes his time in office as turbulent, with regional factionalism constantly threatening his government.

## margaret-ekpo (+204 words)

**early_life:**

> Through her mother she belonged to the royal family of King Eyo Honesty II of Creek Town; her father came from Aguluzigbo in present-day Anambra State. She completed standard six in 1934, but her hopes of training as a teacher ended when her father died that year, and she worked as a pupil-teacher. In 1938 she married a doctor, John Udo Ekpo, an Ibibio, and the couple moved to Aba. In 1946 she studied domestic science in Dublin, Ireland (Wikipedia).

**career:**

> Her first political involvement came in 1945, when she attended meetings in place of her husband, a civil servant, to protest the colonial administration's discrimination against Nigerian doctors; at a rally she was the only woman present to hear Mbonu Ojike, Nnamdi Azikiwe and Herbert Macaulay speak. In the 1950s she joined Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti in protesting the killing of leaders who had been protesting colonial practices at the Enugu coal mine. By 1955 women outnumbered men among Aba's voters. Her political career ended with the civil war, when she was detained by the Biafran authorities for three years (Wikipedia).

**legacy:**

> After the First Republic she took a less prominent role in politics. In 2001 the airport at Calabar was renamed Margaret Ekpo International Airport (Wikipedia).

## amina-of-zazzau (+173 words)

**early_life:**

> According to oral traditions collected by the anthropologist David E. Jones, Amina was brought up at her grandfather's court and instructed by him in political and military matters. At sixteen she was named Magajiya, heir apparent. After her parents died, around 1566, her brother became king, and Amina became known as a leading warrior in his cavalry (Wikipedia).

**career:**

> Zazzau was one of the seven original Hausa states, and before Amina's time already one of the largest. Traditions say that soon after becoming queen she began a 34-year campaign of expansion to the south-east and south-west, since Kano, Katsina and the Jos Plateau blocked the way north and east, with an army of 20,000 foot soldiers and 1,000 cavalry, conquering lands as far as Kwararafa and Nupe. To protect her lands she is said to have surrounded her towns with earthen walls; many survive and are known as ganuwar Amina, "Amina's walls". The circumstances of her death are uncertain: one tradition says she died in battle at Attaagar, probably near Idah (Wikipedia).

## johnson-aguiyi-ironsi (+152 words)

**early_life:**

> He was born to Igbo Catholic parents; his father was Ezeugo Aguiyi, and he took the name Johnson from his brother-in-law, who served as a father figure to him. At eighteen, against his sister's wishes, he joined the Nigeria Regiment (Wikipedia).

**career:**

> He joined the Nigeria Regiment as a private in 1942 and became a company sergeant major in 1946, when he was also sent for officer training at Camberley, England. He was commissioned in the Royal West African Frontier Force in 1949, served as an equerry to Queen Elizabeth II on her visit to Nigeria in 1956, and became a major in 1958 and commander of the 5th Battalion in Kano in 1960. On 1 May 1965 he succeeded the last British officer, Sir Christopher Welby-Everard, as head of the Nigerian army (Wikipedia; Wikipedia's history of Nigeria). For his service as equerry he was made a Member of the Royal Victorian Order.

## moshood-abiola (+160 words)

**early_life:**

> He was the son of a cocoa trader and a kola-nut trader in Abeokuta. He was his father's twenty-third child but the first to survive infancy, hence the name Kashimawo, "let us wait and see". At nine he began selling firewood before school to help his family, and at fifteen founded a band whose earnings paid for his schooling at Baptist Boys' High School, Abeokuta, where he edited the school magazine with Olusegun Obasanjo as his deputy. At nineteen he joined the NCNC (Wikipedia).

**career:**

> After working for the Nigerian subsidiary of Pfizer, he joined ITT Corporation as an accountant; his success in recovering debts owed to the company by the military won him a larger role in the firm. He went on to found many companies, among them Abiola Farms, Abiola Bookshops, Concord Press and Concord Airlines. Through his National Concord newspaper he supported Islamic causes in the 1980s, including Nigeria's entry into the Organisation of the Islamic Conference (Wikipedia).

## jaja-of-opobo (+172 words)

**early_life:**

> In his youth Jaja was kidnapped from Igboland, possibly by the Aro confederacy, and sold into slavery in Bonny. He earned his way out of slavery after years of service to his master, and on his master's death took charge of his trade and rose to head the Anna Pepple House (Wikipedia).

**historical_significance:**

> Jaja shipped palm oil directly to Liverpool, sent his children to school in Glasgow, built a secular school in Opobo and barred missionaries from his kingdom. At the Berlin Conference of 1884 Opobo was assigned to Britain, and when Jaja refused to stop taxing British traders, the vice-consul Henry Hamilton Johnston invited him to negotiations in 1887, at which he was seized. After his exile and death the power of Opobo declined quickly. A memorial to him was erected in the centre of Opobo town in 1903 (Wikipedia). His son, Prince Waribo, was sent to school at Frodsham in Cheshire, England, where he died in 1882, aged 13 or 14; his grave is in the churchyard of St Laurence's Church.

## shehu-shagari (+154 words)

**early_life:**

> The town of Shagari was founded by his great-grandfather, Ahmadu Rufa'i. His father, Aliyu, a farmer, trader and herder, became the village head (Magajin Shagari) and died when Shehu was five (Wikipedia).

**education:**

> He began in a Quranic school, attended Yabo elementary school from 1931 to 1935, middle school in Sokoto from 1936 to 1940, and Barewa College from 1941 to 1944, and then trained at the Teachers' Training College in Zaria. From 1953 to 1958 he was a visiting teacher in Sokoto Province, and from 1954 to 1958 a member of the Federal Scholarship Board. As minister of economic development he helped draw up Nigeria's post-independence national economic plan (Wikipedia). He died on 28 December 2018 at the National Hospital, Abuja. His father had given up some of his trading interests on becoming village head, because tradition barred rulers from business. Shagari married three wives, Amina, Aisha and Hadiza, and had several children (Wikipedia).

## idris-alooma (+144 words)

**early_life:**

> Idris was a son of Mai Ali II Zainami; his mother, Amsa Aliram, was probably the daughter of a Bilala ruler. After his father's short reign the throne passed to relatives, and then to Aissa Koli, who ruled with the title magira (queen mother) until Idris could take the throne and then gave way to him. Some traditions say she raised him in secret, building him a brick palace at Gambaru near the Yobe River, far from the capital, Ngazargamu (Wikipedia).

**legacy:**

> According to tradition, Idris was killed near present-day Maiduguri by an archer hidden in a tree, and was buried in Lake Alau, from which his name Alooma, or Alau-ma, is said to come. He also sought negotiations with the Ottoman Empire over Fezzan, an important trans-Saharan trading centre (Wikipedia). Before his reign Bornu had suffered famines, raids by its neighbours and internal conflict.

## muhammad-al-amin-al-kanemi (+140 words)

**early_life:**

> Al-Kanemi was born in Murzuk in 1776. His father was a Kanembu Islamic teacher from near Mao in Kanem, and his mother the daughter of an Arab trader. He learned the Quran in Murzuk and studied under scholars elsewhere, including Tripoli. In the 1790s he went on pilgrimage to Mecca with his father, who died in Medina, and stayed in the Middle East for about a decade to continue his studies. On his way home he settled at Ngala, where he gathered many students and married a daughter of its ruler (Wikipedia). While he was in Ngala the Sokoto jihad, launched in Hausaland in 1804, reached Bornu by 1807; Mai Ahmad wrote to Usman dan Fodio and Muhammad Bello asking the cause of the attacks, and in 1808 the Fulani captured the capital, Ngazargamu, forcing the mai to flee (Wikipedia).

## ahmadu-bello (+140 words)

**early_life:**

> He was a descendant of Usman dan Fodio, a great-grandson of Caliph Muhammad Bello and a grandson of Caliph Atiku na Raba. He received an Islamic education at home and then attended Sokoto Provincial School and Katsina Training College (now Barewa College), finishing in 1931, and taught English at Sokoto Middle School. In 1934 he became district head of Rabah, and in 1938 divisional head of Gusau and a member of the Sultan's council. That year he sought to become Sultan of Sokoto but lost to Siddiq Abubakar III, who made him Sardauna of Sokoto; by 1944 he was chief secretary of the Sokoto Native Administration (Wikipedia). In the 1940s he joined Jam'iyyar Mutanen Arewa, which became the Northern People's Congress in 1951, and in 1948 he went to England on a government scholarship to study local government administration (Wikipedia).

## nana-olomu (+153 words)

**early_life:**

> Nana was born at Jakpa on the Benin River, probably in 1852, one of the younger sons of Olomu, who founded Ebrohimi and became the leading Itsekiri merchant on the river; his mother, Memese, was an Urhobo woman from Evhro (Effurun). By the early 1870s he spoke English and acted as his father's deputy. When Olomu died in 1883 he arranged, unusually, for Nana to inherit the trading organisation intact, with its connections, canoes, weapons and large enslaved workforce (Wikipedia).

**legacy:**

> Nana is popularly remembered in Nigeria as a forerunner of anti-colonial resistance, while historians also place his resistance within the power structures of the palm-oil trade, in which his organisation combined regional alliances with monopoly, armed coercion and enslaved labour (Wikipedia). His former residence at Koko houses the Nanna Living History Museum and was declared a national monument in 1990; objects seized in 1894 are held by the National Maritime Museum in Greenwich.

## chukwuemeka-odumegwu-ojukwu (+122 words)

**early_life:**

> After Oxford he returned to Nigeria as an administrative officer, serving at Udi and later at Umuahia and Aba between 1955 and 1957. In 1957 he applied to join the Nigerian Army, then a small force in which few commissioned officers had gone beyond primary school (Wikipedia).

**education:**

> His father, the son of a clan chief from Nnewi, sent him to school in Lagos, at St Patrick's School, CMS Grammar School and, from 1944, King's College. In 1946 he went to Epsom College in England, where he stayed for six years and excelled in sports as well as academics. At Oxford he first studied law, as his father wished, before switching to modern history, and he joined the West African Students' Union (Wikipedia).

## chinua-achebe (+152 words)

**education:**

> He went to secondary school at Government College Umuahia, in present-day Abia State, and was among the first intake of University College, Ibadan (now the University of Ibadan), then an associate college of the University of London, where he was given a bursary to study medicine. At Ibadan he became fiercely critical of the way Western literature depicted Africa (Wikipedia).

**early_life:**

> Achebe was born at St Simon's Church, Nneobi, near Ogidi, and baptised Albert Chinualumogu. His father, Isaiah Okafo Achebe, was a teacher and evangelist and an early Christian convert in Ogidi; his mother, Janet Anaenechi Iloegbunam, a blacksmith's daughter from Awka, was a leader among church women. Both parents stood at the crossroads of traditional culture and Christian influence, which deeply affected Chinua: his father stopped practising Odinani, the religion of his ancestors, but still respected its traditions under the influence of his uncle, a titled leader who refused to convert (Wikipedia).

## funmilayo-ransome-kuti (+134 words)

**education:**

> From 1919 to 1922 she studied at Wincham Hall School for Girls in Cheshire, England, learning elocution, music, dressmaking and French. Before returning to Abeokuta in 1922 she stopped using her Christian names and took her Yoruba name, Funmilayo, meaning "God has given me joy" (Wikipedia).

**early_life:**

> She was born Frances Abigail Olufunmilayo Thomas, the daughter of Daniel Olumeyuwa Thomas, who farmed and traded palm produce, and Lucretia Phyllis Omoyeni Adeosolu, a dressmaker. Her father's family belonged to the Saro community, returnees from Sierra Leone, many of them formerly enslaved Africans freed and resettled in Freetown before returning to Yorubaland in the nineteenth century. Her great-grandmother, Sarah Taiwo, had been captured by slave traders before eventually returning to her family in Abeokuta (Wikipedia). She was also the first woman in Abeokuta to drive a car.

## aminu-kano (+115 words)

**early_life:**

> His family, of the Fulani Gyanawa clan, was known for its learning in Islamic law: his father later became chief alkali (judge) of Kano, and local tradition holds that Usman dan Fodio himself appointed one of his relatives to that office. His mother's family, Fulbe from Kukawa in Borno, also produced scholars, among them a personal adviser to the emir. Of his mother's six children, he was the only one to reach the age of fifteen (Wikipedia). As a student teacher he spent months in Bauchi and Zaria and visited schools in the south, and after qualifying he became a junior teacher at Bauchi Middle School, where his friendship with Abubakar Tafawa Balewa began (Wikipedia).

## muhammadu-buhari (+159 words)

**early_life:**

> Buhari was the twenty-third child of Hardo Adamu, a Fulani chief from Dumurkul, and Zulaihat, of Hausa and Kanuri ancestry. His father died when he was four, and he was brought up under the guardianship of a son of the Emir of Daura. He attended Quranic school, primary school in Daura and Mai'Adua, and Katsina Middle School (later Katsina Provincial Secondary School) from 1956 to 1961, where he was head boy (Wikipedia).

**education:**

> In 1962 he was one of 70 young men recruited into the Nigerian Military Training College, and trained as an officer cadet at Mons Officer Cadet School in Aldershot, England; he was commissioned in January 1963. He commanded the Second Infantry Battalion from 1965 to 1967 and took part in the July 1966 counter-coup with other northern officers (Wikipedia). The Library of Congress country study adds that he was later military governor of the North-Eastern State and federal commissioner for petroleum and mines from 1976 to 1978.

## obafemi-awolowo (+162 words)

**early_life:**

> He was the only son of David Shopolu Awolowo, a farmer and sawyer who was one of the first people of Ikenne to become a Christian, and Mary Efunyela Awolowo; his grandmother was a devout worshipper of Ifá. His father died of smallpox when Obafemi was about eleven. He attended Baptist Boys' High School, Abeokuta, worked as a teacher, shorthand typist, clerk and newspaper correspondent, studied commerce as an external student of the University of London, and went to Britain in 1944 to study law (Wikipedia).

**achievements:**

> In his book Path to Nigerian Freedom (1947), the first systematic federalist manifesto by a Nigerian politician, he argued for federalism as the basis of national integration, and he led the demands that produced the federal constitution of 1954. As premier of the Western Region he introduced free primary education for all and free health care for children, and established the first television service in Africa in 1959, financed largely by the region's cocoa wealth (Wikipedia).

## ken-saro-wiwa (+118 words)

**education:**

> His father, Chief Jim Wiwa, was a forest ranger from Bane in Ogoniland, whose people speak the Khana dialect of Ogoni. After secondary school at Government College Umuahia, where he won prizes in history and English, he studied English at the University of Ibadan on a scholarship, winning departmental prizes in 1963 and 1965 and touring with a drama troupe. He was briefly a teaching assistant at the University of Lagos before lecturing at Nsukka; when the civil war broke out he supported the federal government and returned to Bori (Wikipedia). A radio adaptation of one of his plays was produced in 1972, and in 1985 he produced Basi and Company, a television adaptation that ran until 1990.

## herbert-macaulay (+146 words)

**education:**

> He attended St Paul's Breadfruit School and CMS Faji School in Lagos, and CMS Grammar School, Lagos, from 1877 to 1880; his father died in 1878. After a trading and missionary journey up the Niger with his uncle, he worked as a clerk in the Public Works Department, and in 1890 left for England with the colonial government's support to train as a civil engineer and surveyor in Plymouth (Wikipedia).

**career:**

> He returned to Lagos in 1893 as surveyor of Crown lands, and left the colonial service in 1898. Accounts of his departure differ: Wikipedia records his growing dislike of British rule, but also allegations by his British superiors, cited by the historian Kristin Mann, that he had used his office to help friends obtain Crown grants. He then worked as a private surveyor and became a political activist, joining the Anti-Slavery and Aborigines' Protection Society (Wikipedia).

## murtala-muhammed (+145 words)

**career:**

> He joined the army in 1958, trained briefly in Nigeria and Ghana and as an officer cadet at Sandhurst, and was commissioned as a second lieutenant in the Signals in 1961, serving briefly with a signals troop in the Congo. In 1962 he became aide-de-camp to M. A. Majekodunmi, the federal administrator of the Western Region, and in 1963 took charge of the First Brigade Signal Troop in Kaduna (Wikipedia).

**early_life:**

> His father, Muhammed Risqua, a veterinary officer in the Kano Native Authority, came from a family of Islamic jurists who had served as chief judges in Kano, and was related to Aminu Kano and Inuwa Wada; he died in 1953. His mother, Uwani Rahamatu, was from the Kanuri and Fulani Jobawa clan. He became a brigadier in 1971, at 33 one of the youngest generals in Nigeria, and in 1974 federal commissioner for communications (Wikipedia).

## sani-abacha (+131 words)

**historical_significance:**

> In October 1995, amid growing international isolation and domestic criticism of his government's human-rights record, Abacha established the National Human Rights Commission by decree, at the same time as he announced a new programme of transition to civilian rule (Wikipedia).

**early_life:**

> He trained at the Nigerian Military Training College in Kaduna in 1962 and at Mons Officer Cadet School in Aldershot, England, and was commissioned in 1963. Wikipedia records that he was involved in Nigeria's military coups from July 1966, when, as a second lieutenant in Kaduna, he took part in the counter-coup. He served as a platoon and battalion commander in the civil war, commanded the 2nd Infantry Division from 1975, and in 1983 was general officer commanding the 2nd Mechanised Division and a member of the Supreme Military Council (Wikipedia).

## nnamdi-azikiwe (+131 words)

**early_life:**

> His name, Nnamdi, means "my father is alive" in Igbo. His father, Obed-Edom Chukwuemeka Azikiwe, from Onitsha, was a clerk in the colonial administration, and his mother, Rachel, was descended from a royal family of Onitsha. Concerned that the boy spoke Hausa but not Igbo, his father sent him to Onitsha in 1912 to learn the Igbo language and culture (Wikipedia).

**career:**

> While studying in the United States he wrote against the shooting of women in the Women's War of 1929, and in 1931 published his first book, Liberia in World Politics. He returned to Lagos in 1937 and founded the West African Pilot, with the motto "Show the light and the people will find the way", the flagship of a group of newspapers that spread his nationalist message across Nigeria (Wikipedia).

## usman-dan-fodio (+111 words)

**historical_significance:**

> One of the first acts of his mission was the mass education of women: he criticised scholars for neglecting half of humanity, and taught his daughters as well as his sons. Several of his daughters became scholars and writers, notably Nana Asma'u (Wikipedia).

**education:**

> After studying the Quran with his father, he went to other teachers, among them his relatives Uthman Binduri and Muhammad Sambo, and then Jibril bin Umar, a powerful scholar and an early advocate of jihad, with whom he later disagreed over who should be considered a Muslim. At about twenty he set up his own school at Degel, where he taught and preached while continuing to study (Wikipedia).

## fela-kuti (+92 words)

**early_life:**

> His father, Israel Oludotun Ransome-Kuti, was an Anglican minister, school principal and the first president of the Nigeria Union of Teachers; his brothers Beko and Olikoye became well-known doctors. He was a cousin of the Nobel Prize-winning writer Wole Soyinka: both descended from Josiah Ransome-Kuti, an Anglican clergyman and musical pioneer. He attended Abeokuta Grammar School, and in 1958 went to London to study music at Trinity College of Music, with the trumpet as his preferred instrument; there he formed the band Koola Lobitos, playing a fusion of jazz and highlife (Wikipedia).

## ovonramwen (+84 words)

**early_life:**

> Before his father's death, Idugbowa had been dissatisfied that he had not been formally installed as Edaiken (crown prince). His accession was followed by rivalry between established palace chiefs and his own supporters, by executions and purges at court, and by provincial disputes (Wikipedia).

**legacy:**

> On his way into exile he was photographed by the Nigerian photographer Jonathan Adagogo Green. The images became widely reproduced records of the conquest, interpreted both as colonial pictures of defeat and within West African traditions of dignified portraiture (Wikipedia).

## idia-iyoba-of-benin (+87 words)

**historical_significance:**

> Unlike most titled chiefs, an Iyoba could commission works in ivory and copper alloy for devotional and personal use, including ikegobo (altars to the hand) and rectangular altarpieces. Idia's role is still remembered in Benin ritual: the Emobo ceremony, in which the oba performs with an ivory gong, is interpreted as commemorating both Esigie's return and the protection given by Idia's followers (Wikipedia). In the Igala–Benin war of 1515–1516, her head slave and his men were credited with killing the commander of the Igala forces.
